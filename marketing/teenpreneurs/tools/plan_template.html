Big idea

The old mould is gone. Make something new.

Your brief is the right one, and the site already holds the strongest line: most schools teach you to answer other people's questions well. Teenpreneurs teaches you to ask your own, and act on them. The campaign puts that line next to the one fact everyone feels: work is changing.

Headline options

  • Ask your own questions.
  • Not made to blend in. Made to create.
  • Differentability: material, not a problem.
  • Too much for school? Just right for this.
  • AI can answer. Your teen can decide.
  • Jobs are changing. Be the one who starts something.

Six message pillars

  • The world is changing. Employers expect 170M new jobs and 92M displaced by 2030 (WEF).
  • Agency over obedience. From waiting to be told, to seeing a problem and starting.
  • Think sideways. For teens who are too much, or not enough, for the old mould, including neurodivergent teens.
  • Proof, not certificates. A Portfolio of Proof they can show.
  • A global room. Teens from different countries, one weekly rhythm.
  • AI as a thinking partner. Never a shortcut around thinking.

One guardrail from your own site: "We won't sell you urgency." So the AI message is about readiness and curiosity, not fear. Scarcity is allowed because it is true: 24 seats, and the founding price applies to the first edition only.

Three parent personas to write for

The worried planner

Reads about AI and jobs, wants her teen ready. Needs evidence and safety: Portfolio of Proof, mentors named, refund policy.

The parent of a square peg

Teen is creative, intense, bored or sensitive, and often neurodivergent, ADHD most of all. Needs to hear "that is material, not a problem". Your manifesto and Antek's story speak straight to this parent.

The homeschool curator

Builds a patchwork of classes. Needs it to add to what they do, fit a weekly slot and not eat the week. "It doesn't replace school" is the line.

Neurodivergent teens, ADHD first

This is your warmest audience, and you belong to it. The foreword of your own journal already says it in print: "I didn't fit the mould. I have ADHD, dyslexia, and the kind of brain that never quite sits still." You are also, in the words of the author page, a home educator of two teenagers, so you are inside the homeschool world you are selling to. That lived experience is what competitors cannot copy. The school site already says many students have ADHD and that small groups, patient teachers and no grading meet them where they are. The unickorns.com site tells the differentability story but does not yet say the ADHD part. That is your call. Antek's story is separate from yours and stays his decision.

Lived experience is the lead story

Milly's voice

A weekly one minute series, "Differentability with Milly", unscripted and in first person. I will not write your story. Here are prompts to answer in your own words:

  • When did you first feel like a square peg, and what did you do about it?
  • What did trying to fit in cost you?
  • What is one thing about your ADHD that you now use to run a company?
  • What did you try to fix that turned out to be a strength?
  • What would you tell a 14 year old who feels "too much"?
  • What does differentability mean to you, in one sentence?

Antek's part

He is a minor, so the choices are his and yours to make together. The options run from music only, to music plus a short chat, to nothing about ADHD at all. Whatever he picks:

  • He approves every cut before it goes live.
  • He can withdraw a video at any time.
  • Comments are moderated, because public teens attract unkind replies.
  • He is not used in paid ads without his specific yes.
  • He is a musician first. No "inspiring despite" framing.

Ground rules

  • First person is the strongest and safest: "I have ADHD". Never tell viewers what they have.
  • "This is my experience, not medical advice." Say it, and never advise on medication.
  • Show the cost as well as the win. Honesty is why it works.
  • Skip "ADHD superpower". Say what actually helps.
  • Your experience is a credential for empathy, not a clinical one. Keep claims at that level.

A draft bio line, only if you want it: "Home educator of two teenagers. Founder of uNickorns. Lover of dogs and underdogs. Learned to build with ADHD and dyslexia." It also opens a door to ADHD, homeschool and entrepreneurship podcasts, where "a founder with ADHD, and her teenage guitarist son" is a story hosts will want.

What we can say

  • Big ideas need somewhere to go. Here they get built, tested and finished in small steps.
  • A weekly rhythm that moves: Meet, Think, Build, Test, Reflect. No grades, no lectures.
  • Small groups of up to 12, and a mentor who knows your teen's name.
  • Intensity, sensitivity and an unusual pace are information, not problems.
  • Honest "not for everyone" lines: live and weekly, needs some English, not therapy.

What we must not say

  • Any promise to treat, fix or improve ADHD or any condition. This is not therapy.
  • "Is your child ADHD?" in paid ads. Meta and other platforms ban ads that imply you know a person's health condition, and they restrict targeting by it.
  • Diagnosis as a hook aimed at strangers. Paid ads talk about ideas, focus and strengths. Your own first person story is different, and organic posts and groups can name ADHD.
  • Superpower slogans or before and after stories. Families see through them.
  • Any adjustment we do not truly offer.

Content that fits

"Hyperfocus on what you love" skits, 10 minute project sprints, a teen explaining how they finish things, and a parent Q and A with Milly on what her classes learned from teaching students with ADHD.

Gaps between the site's promise and the book

  • AI. The campaign leads with AI and the site promises "use AI well", but the journal never mentions it. A short chapter or digital insert, "Think alongside AI", would close that.
  • Projects and the Portfolio of Proof. Chapter 7 has a business idea box, but there are no pages for the cycle the site describes: idea, first version, feedback, what failed, what changed, what I learned. A project log insert would make the book a true companion.
  • Teamwork and money basics. Both are promised on the site, and both are thin in the book.

One tension to manage

Chapter 1 says the system "wasn't built for you" and calls school an industrial era factory design. The site says Teenpreneurs does not replace school. My advice: keep the sharper lines for organic content and homeschool audiences, and use the site's gentler wording in paid ads. Historians also dispute how much of the factory story is true, so a public ad that states it as fact invites a pile on. "Shaped in the industrial era" is safer.

Guardrails

Keep it trustworthy

  • Ben and Antek are minors. Get written consent, keep accounts owned by an adult, and share only what they are happy with.
  • No invented testimonials, student counts or seat numbers. Every number on a post must match the site and the real enrolment.
  • No medical or therapeutic claims. Never promise to help, treat or improve ADHD or any condition. Paid ads stay on ideas and strengths.
  • No income or job promises. Claims stay at confidence, agency and evidence of work.
  • Scarcity must be true. Update the seat count the same day it changes.
  • Ask homeschool group admins before posting. Many groups ban promotion, and a good post removed is worse than none.
  • Email only with opt in. Your form has an optional box for updates, so build the list from that.
